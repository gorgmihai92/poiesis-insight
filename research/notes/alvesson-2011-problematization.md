## What it says
- Most research questions are made by finding or constructing a gap in the literature. That leaves the literature's assumptions unchallenged (p. 1, 3).
- Problematization is proposed as an addition to gap-spotting, not a replacement: the two are not mutually exclusive (p. 5, 6).
- Five types of assumption can be contested, from narrow to broad (p. 8–9):
  - **in-house**: shared and accepted as unproblematic within one school of thought (p. 8);
  - **root metaphor**: the broader image of the subject matter (p. 9);
  - **paradigm**: the ontological, epistemological and methodological assumptions under a literature (p. 9);
  - **ideology**: political, moral and gender-related assumptions about the subject matter (p. 9);
  - **field**: shared by several schools, sometimes across paradigms and disciplines (p. 9).
- A challenge to a broad assumption can be superficial, and a good challenge to a narrow one can matter (p. 9).
- Six principles, iterative and not a recipe (p. 10): identify a domain of literature; identify and articulate its assumptions; evaluate them; develop an alternative assumption ground; consider it in relation to its audience; evaluate the alternative.
- The domain is read narrowly and in depth: a path-defining study, one or a few authoritative summaries, or a few recent influential pieces (p. 10).
- How each type is found (p. 10–11): in-house, from the internal debates of a group of authors who cite each other; root metaphor, by naming the image behind a text and producing a confronting one; paradigm, from familiarity with another world view; ideology, from positions with other interests and values; field, from what opposed schools have in common and do not debate, and from other fields.
- Not every assumption is worth contesting. The test is what could be learned by challenging it; evidence that an assumption is problematic counts, though assumptions can seldom be tested directly (p. 12).
- The alternative has to go beyond counter-positions that already exist (p. 12).
- An alternative is judged by its audience's likely response: "that's obvious", "it's absurd" or "that's interesting". The aim is the third, between the first two, and it is to be tried on people from that audience (p. 13).
- Limits the authors state: overproblematization tires a field and yields few positive results (p. 20); the method does not guarantee a good outcome (p. 21); whether its questions lead to more interesting research than gap-spotting is expected, with "no guarantees" (p. 20).

## What it means for the plugin
- The `questions` playbook contests the assumptions behind a gap on these five types, after the gap is described.
- What comes out is a question, never a conclusion: the paper's criterion is "interesting", not "true".
- The steps are prompts for thinking and are not all required. A question may still come from a plain gap.
- Not usable as evidence that the method works: it was shown on one article, in management studies.
